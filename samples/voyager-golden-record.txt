In 1977, NASA launched two spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, to study the outer planets. Bolted to each one is a gold-plated copper phonograph record, a kind of time capsule intended for anyone, or anything, that might find it drifting through interstellar space millions of years from now.

The record holds greetings in fifty-five languages, from Akkadian to Wu Chinese, along with sounds of Earth: thunder, wind, whale songs, a mother's kiss, and ninety minutes of music ranging from Bach and Beethoven to Chuck Berry and traditional songs from Peru, Bulgaria, and Papua New Guinea. It also carries encoded images showing our solar system, human anatomy, and daily life, along with instructions, in symbols alone, on how to build a player.

Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in 2012 and is now the most distant human-made object, still sending data back across more than twenty billion kilometers, carrying its message long after everyone who built it is gone.
