In the summer of 1977, NASA launched two robotic spacecraft, Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, on a grand tour of the outer solar system. Their instruments were built to study Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune, but their most famous cargo isn't a scientific instrument at all. Bolted to the outside of each probe is a gold-plated copper phonograph record, twelve inches across, meant to survive essentially forever in the vacuum of space and to speak, if anyone or anything ever finds it, on behalf of all of humanity.

The Voyager record had a predecessor. Five years earlier, NASA had launched Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11, each carrying a small gold-anodized aluminum plaque designed by Carl Sagan and Frank Drake: etched line drawings of a man and a woman, a diagram of our solar system, and a map of pulsars marking our location in the galaxy. The plaques proved that a simple physical message could ride along with a spacecraft into deep space, and by the time Voyager was being designed, Sagan's team was ready to attempt something far more ambitious than etched lines on metal.

The mission itself existed because of a rare planetary alignment. Once every 176 years, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune line up in a way that lets a spacecraft use each planet's gravity to slingshot on to the next, cutting a trip that would otherwise take decades down to about a dozen years. Voyager 2 remains, to this day, the only spacecraft ever to visit Uranus and Neptune, and its flight path through all four giant planets in a single mission has never been repeated.

Along the way, the two probes carried out one of the most productive missions in the history of planetary science. They revealed active volcanoes erupting on Jupiter's moon Io, hinted at a hidden ocean beneath the icy crust of Europa, and resolved the fine structure and braided strands of Saturn's rings. At Uranus, Voyager 2 found a magnetic field bizarrely tilted almost sideways relative to the planet's spin. At Neptune, it discovered the fastest winds ever measured in the solar system and a giant storm called the Great Dark Spot, and found geysers of nitrogen ice erupting from the moon Triton, whose backward orbit suggests it was once a captured object from the icy Kuiper Belt. In 1990, at Sagan's urging, Voyager 1 turned its camera back toward home from nearly six billion kilometers away and took the photograph known as "Pale Blue Dot," showing Earth as a single, barely visible speck of light suspended in a sunbeam.

Back on Earth, the golden record was assembled in about a year by a committee chaired by Sagan, working with Ann Druyan, Frank Drake, Timothy Ferris, and the artist Jon Lomberg. They had to distill the entire human experience onto a single disc, a task that meant constant, often painful choices about what to include and what to leave out. Ann Druyan, who later married Sagan, spent weeks compiling a list of music from around the world before the final ninety minutes were chosen; she has said that falling in love with Sagan during that process is itself woven into the record, since the team recorded her brainwaves and heartbeat for one of its encoded sounds.

Encoded onto the record are 116 images, transmitted as analog signals that a sufficiently advanced civilization could decode into pictures: diagrams of our solar system and of DNA, photographs of human anatomy and daily life, of forests, deserts, and city streets, of a mother nursing a child, and of people eating, building, and looking up at the stars.

Alongside the images is a collection nicknamed "Sounds of Earth": surf, wind, and thunder, birds, whales, and crickets, footsteps and laughter, a mother's kiss, a baby's cry, the clatter of tools, a heartbeat, a rocket launch, and Morse code. There are spoken greetings in fifty-five languages, from Akkadian, a language extinct for millennia, to Wu Chinese, along with a message from then President Jimmy Carter and one from United Nations Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim. Ninety minutes of music round out the audio: Bach and Beethoven alongside Chuck Berry's "Johnny B. Goode," Blind Willie Johnson's "Dark Was the Night, Cold Was the Ground," Peruvian panpipes, a Navajo night chant, Azerbaijani bagpipe music, and Javanese gamelan, chosen to represent as much of the range of human culture as ninety minutes could possibly hold.

The record's cover carries no words, only symbols: a diagram showing how to play it, the correct speed of rotation, and a map of pulsars pointing back to the location of our solar system and the approximate date of launch. A small sample of purified uranium-238 is also mounted on the cover; its slow, steady radioactive decay works like a clock, letting any future finder calculate how long the record has been drifting since it left Earth.

Keeping the mission itself alive has become its own kind of engineering challenge. Each Voyager's onboard computer has a tiny fraction of the processing power of a modern key fob, and commands now take more than twenty hours to reach Voyager 1 at the speed of light, with the reply taking another twenty to arrive back on Earth. To operate the spacecraft today, NASA engineers have had to track down decades-old documentation, and in some cases relearn retired programming languages that almost no one uses anymore, just to keep 1970s hardware running at the edge of interstellar space.

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the sun's influence gives way to interstellar space, in 2012; Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Both are still transmitting, now more than twenty billion kilometers from Earth, though their plutonium power sources are fading and are expected to drop below the threshold needed to run any instruments within the next several years. After that, both spacecraft will fall silent and drift, unpowered, carrying their gold records through the emptiness of interstellar space, orbiting the center of the Milky Way, for hundreds of millions or even billions of years, long after every trace of human civilization on Earth has disappeared.

Despite decades of public fascination, NASA never officially released the record's contents for people on Earth to actually hear. That changed in 2016, when a small label called Ozma Records ran a crowdfunding campaign to press an authorized vinyl reissue of the complete Golden Record; it became one of the most successful vinyl reissue campaigns ever funded, finally letting people listen to the exact message their species had sent into the stars.

The chance that either record is ever actually found and understood is vanishingly small; interstellar space is almost entirely empty, and neither spacecraft is aimed at any particular star. Carl Sagan himself described the record's launch as a hopeful act regardless of the odds: a bottle cast into the cosmic ocean, saying something about our world, sent at a time when the spacecraft could be assured of at least a billion years of silent travel before, if ever, another shore is reached. In that sense the record was always a message for two audiences at once: some far-future finder who may never exist, and us, here and now, forced to decide what we would say about ourselves if we only got to say it once.
